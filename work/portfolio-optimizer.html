Portfolio Construction Workbench

Seven allocators and six covariance estimators on the same inputs, every answer checked by its KKT residual.

Mean-variance optimization has a famous failure mode: it bets hardest on the directions it estimated worst. So the interesting question isn’t what the tangency portfolio is. It’s how much of a frontier is real, and whether one backtest can tell you which allocator wins. This workbench is built to answer those.

Optimal, and provably so

Every optimizer reduces to repeated projection onto a box-constrained budget. I solve that projection exactly, in O(n log n), instead of bisecting to a tolerance. It runs thousands of times per solve, and the exact version made the whole efficient frontier about 50× faster. Each solution then reports its KKT residual, about 1e-10 to 3e-10 across universes from 6 to 100 assets.

That certificate earned its keep. The first version used Markowitz’s Critical Line Algorithm, which looked plausible but broke its own 20% weight cap. A random search beat it, the residual made the bug undeniable, and it was replaced.

Estimation error, measured

A backtest is one sample path, so it mixes estimation error with luck. The workbench also draws fresh samples from a known distribution, allocates on each, and scores the weights under the true parameters. Thirty two-year trials:

Spread is the standard deviation of achieved Sharpe across trials.

Maximum Sharpe has the highest ceiling and the largest shortfall, with four times the spread of risk parity. Equal weight has a spread of exactly zero, because it uses no estimates.

How it’s tested

72 tests on properties rather than golden numbers. The walk-forward backtest is checked for lookahead by replacing every return after the final rebalance with garbage: every weight in the path has to stay bit-identical. Risk-parity contributions must equal 1/N to machine precision, which is the test that caught a budget-constraint bug in the first implementation.

Limits

Constraints are box plus budget only, with no turnover or sector limits. Expected returns are a single vector, and the estimation study assumes a stationary true model, which is the most flattering assumption for every method in it.
