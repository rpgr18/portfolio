An end-to-end deep-learning system for interpreting marine seismic surveys, automating two of the most time-consuming tasks in offshore geophysics: fault detection and horizon tracing.

Background

A marine seismic survey images the geology beneath the ocean floor. A vessel emits controlled acoustic signals that travel through the water and the rock below; where material properties such as density and elasticity change, part of the signal reflects back and is recorded by towed sensors. Surveys are collected in 2D (a single plane), 3D (a full volume) or 4D (3D repeated over time).

The data drives offshore exploration, CO₂ sequestration planning and geohazard assessment, and it is large: hundreds of thousands of traces per line, stored as SEG-Y. Interpreting it means identifying horizons (layer boundaries) and faults (fractures where layers break and shift), work usually done by hand by trained geophysicists. It’s slow, expensive and subjective.

Fault detection

Faults are found on 2D NAMSS survey lines by a three-stage pipeline that pairs a classical detector with a deep-learning surrogate.

  • Classical detector. Four fault-sensitive attributes are extracted from each section, NCC coherence (weighted 35%), horizontal gradient (25%), structure-tensor eigenvalues (25%) and dip variance (15%), then fused into a probability map. Simulated annealing traces fault paths through it.
  • Automated labeling. The classical detector runs across dozens of NAMSS lines, and its fault paths become COCO-format bounding boxes: 168 training images with 1,668 fault annotations, and no manual labeling.
  • CNN surrogate. A Faster R-CNN with a ResNet-50 and Feature Pyramid Network backbone and custom anchor ratios for fault geometry, trained on those labels for 90 epochs (about nine hours on a rented RTX 5090). It reproduces the classical output about 100× faster, 40 seconds for 203 images against one to two hours per image, at an F1 of 0.60 (56.6% precision, 62.5% recall).
  • Explainability. EigenCAM heatmaps over the trained detector show which regions it attends to, confirming it relies on geologically meaningful features rather than memorized patterns.

Horizon detection

A custom 2D CNN, written from scratch in PyTorch, traces the seafloor horizon through the Netherlands F3 Block, a 3D volume of more than 600,000 traces with expert-picked horizons. Asymmetric pooling compresses depth while preserving trace width, matching how a geophysicist scans a section by eye. It reaches a 5.6 m mean absolute error, with 95.4% of predictions within ±15 m of the expert picks, and a noise-robustness study measures how that error grows as noise is added.

A tour of the project

Data

Fault detection uses 2D lines from the USGS National Archive of Marine Seismic Surveys (NAMSS); horizon detection uses the Netherlands F3 Block, an open 3D survey from SEG with expert-picked horizons. A SEG-Y file is a collection of traces, each a 1D amplitude array with metadata such as sampling rate and coordinates. The 2D lines are read with ObsPy and the F3 volume with segyio’s memory-mapped access.
